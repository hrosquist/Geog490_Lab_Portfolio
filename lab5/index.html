Geography of Interest

For this lab, I used two main geographies of interest. For the cross state metropolitan boundary section, I selected the Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro, OR-WA metropolitan statistical area. This region is appropriate because it crosses the Oregon-Washington state line and includes a large urban population centered around Portland, Oregon and Vancouver, Washington. Using this area allowed me to compare census tracts from two different states within one shared metropolitan boundary. For the water-erasing workflow and hotspot analysis, I used King County and the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metropolitan area in Washington. King County is useful for the erase_water() workflow because it contains significant water features, including Puget Sound, Lake Washington, and other major water bodies. The Seattle metropolitan area is also useful for hotspot analysis because income patterns vary strongly across the region, especially between central Seattle, the Eastside, and more suburban or lower-income areas.

Figure 3: Getis-Ord Hotspot Analysis

Figure 3. Getis-Ord hotspot analysis of median household income in the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metro area.

This map shows a Getis-Ord Gi* hotspot analysis of median household income in the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metropolitan area using ACS 2020 5 year estimates from table B19013. The map categorizes tracts into high-income clusters, low-income clusters, and areas that are not statistically significant. High-income clusters represent areas where high median household income values are located near other high-income values. Low-income clusters represent areas where lower-income tracts are located near other lower-income tracts.

Hotspot Analysis Trends

The Getis-Ord hotspot analysis shows clear spatial clustering in median household income across the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metropolitan area. High-income clusters are likely to appear in areas such as Seattle’s wealthier neighborhoods and the Eastside, including places near Bellevue, Redmond, Kirkland, and Mercer Island. These areas tend to have higher household incomes because they are close to major employment centers, especially technology and professional-sector jobs. They also often have higher housing costs, higher property values, and greater access to economic resources.

Low-income clusters are more likely to appear in parts of south Seattle, south King County, and portions of Pierce County. These areas may reflect more affordable housing patterns, older suburban development, lower average wages, and greater racial and economic inequality across the region. The pattern suggests that income is not randomly distributed across the metro area. Instead, similar income levels tend to group together spatially. This likely reflects the combined effects of housing markets, transportation access, job location, historic patterns of segregation, and uneven investment across neighborhoods. Overall, the hotspot analysis helps show where income advantage and disadvantage are geographically concentrated within the Seattle metropolitan region.
